Consent Form

The Effectiveness of Virtual Cockpit Environments to Evaluate Visual Detection of Small Unmanned Aircraft Systems for Collision Avoidance

Key Information

Study Purpose: To learn how pilots visually detect small unmanned aircraft (drones) in a virtual cockpit, and whether that virtual cockpit is a good stand-in for actual flight.

Major Procedures of the Study: Watching recorded approach scenarios through a virtual reality headset, reporting any drone you see with a controller button press and a point of the controller while also saying aloud what you notice, two short questionnaires, and a brief interview.

Duration of Participation: One in-person session of about two hours, with an optional written follow-up a few weeks later that takes about ten to fifteen minutes.

Significant Risks: Mild, short-lived discomfort that some people feel when wearing a virtual reality headset, such as eyestrain, queasiness, or dizziness.

Potential Benefits: There is no direct benefit to you. Your participation helps researchers understand how pilots spot small drones, which matters for everyone who shares the airspace.

Compensation: None. Taking part is voluntary and unpaid.

Background Information

We invite you to participate in a research study on how pilots visually detect small unmanned aircraft, often called drones, in a simulated cockpit. This form will tell you what the study is about, what we will ask you to do, and how your information will be used. Please read this carefully. Feel free to ask questions if you don't understand something. Taking part in this study is completely your choice. You can decide not to join, or if you join, you can stop at any time. There is no punishment or penalty if you decide not to take part or if you quit later. Your decision whether to participate in this study will not affect your employment, your pilot certification, or your standing with any airport, employer, or Oklahoma State University.

This study is being conducted by: Jason Beim, a doctoral student in the School of Educational Foundations, Leadership, and Aviation at Oklahoma State University, and supervised by his faculty adviser, Dr. Jon Loffi, of the same school.

Procedures

If you decide to join, we will ask you to:

1. Fill out a short survey about your flying experience and a brief questionnaire about how you are feeling, which simply helps us keep an eye on your comfort during the session.

2. Read a one-page briefing and let us know you understand it.

3. Put on a virtual reality headset. You will first see a short takeoff, about 25 seconds long, so you can get used to the view, and afterwards be asked whether you noticed anything unusual during it. Then you will watch twelve recorded approach scenarios, each about two minutes long. As you watch, scan the scene the way you would on any approach. When you see a drone, press the controller trigger right away, then point the controller at where you saw it and press again; the study software records the time and pointing direction of each press under your participant code. Also say aloud whatever you notice.

4. Take a short break partway through, when we will check in again about how you are feeling.

5. Sit down for a short interview, about 30 to 45 minutes, about how real the environment felt and what the task was like for you. After the interview you may put the headset back on for a short recorded debriefing and a short video that shows where the drones were.

So that your scanning stays natural and reflects how you really fly, we will not tell you ahead of time which scenarios contain something to spot, how many do, or when. This is necessary so the study results reflect natural scanning behavior. In practice, this means you do nothing differently; you simply observe each approach and note what you see, just as you would on any flight. There are no trick questions or right-or-wrong answers, and your individual performance is not being scored. We are not keeping the study's goal from you; it is the detection of small drones, as described above, and we will go over it with you again at the end of the session. We just do not want to tip off the individual scenarios, because knowing the answers in advance would change how you look.

It will take about two hours, all in one visit. A few weeks later, we will send you a short summary of the main themes from your own interview and invite you to confirm it, correct it, or pull anything you would rather leave out. That follow-up is optional and takes about ten to fifteen minutes.

We will be recording audio of the interview portion only, and only with your separate permission below. If you would rather not be recorded, you are still welcome to take part, and the researcher will simply take written notes instead.

Benefits and Risks of Being in the Study

You may not receive direct benefits from this study, but your participation could help researchers better understand how pilots detect small drones and could help others in the future.

This study involves the following risks:

Privacy Risks: There is a chance your privacy could be at risk, but there are steps in place to keep your information safe. These steps are explained later in this form.

Mild Discomfort from the Headset: Some people feel temporary symptoms when wearing a virtual reality headset, such as eyestrain, mild queasiness, or dizziness. We will take steps to reduce this risk by asking about any history of motion or simulator sickness before we schedule you, checking how you are feeling before, during, and after the session, building in a break partway through, and ending the session if you show or tell us about any discomfort. You can take the headset off and stop at any time. We also ask that you not drive or fly an aircraft right after the session; a quiet place to rest is available, and we will mention this when we schedule you so you can plan your ride. If you do not feel ready to drive when the session ends, let the researcher know, and we will help arrange transportation for you.

If you get hurt or sick because of this study, the researcher will help you get emergency medical care, and emergency services can be reached from the study location. However, Oklahoma State University will not provide money to compensate you for any injury or illness.

Will You Get Paid?

You will not be paid for participating. There is no payment, gift, or other incentive for taking part, just our thanks.

How Will Your Information Be Kept Private?

The information you share will be kept private. We will assign a code or fake name to your information. Your name will not appear in any reports. A list connecting your real name to the code will be stored in a safe place, separate from all other study data, and only the researcher will be able to open it. We will destroy that list once it is no longer needed, which we expect to be after the data analysis and interview review are finished, by mid-2027.

Sometimes, we may not be able to keep your information completely private. For example, in interviews or other situations, others may know what you shared. The Researcher will do everything possible to keep your information private, but we cannot promise total confidentiality. Your name will not be used in any publications or reports. If you happen to mention something that could identify you, such as where you work or your home airport, we will remove or generalize it.

How Your Information Will Be Collected and Stored

We will collect your information using questionnaires, a log of your controller responses recorded by the study software (button-press times and pointing directions, stored under your participant code only), an audio recording of the interview, and notes the researcher takes during the session. Your information will be stored on a password-protected, encrypted computer, with an encrypted backup kept on a drive in a locked cabinet. Paper forms and signed consent documents will be kept in a locked drawer.

Audio recordings will be turned into written text (transcribed) using a transcription service within a software program called MAXQDA. To create the transcript, the audio file is labeled only with your participant code and carries no name or other identifying label. Under the company's license terms, it is used only to produce your transcript and for nothing else. The written transcript is then stored on the researcher's password-protected, encrypted computer and checked against the original recording for accuracy. Before analysis begins, any identifying details you may have mentioned are removed. If you agree to be recorded but would prefer that your audio not be sent to MAXQDA, let us know, and the researcher will transcribe your interview personally instead. Once the recording has been transcribed and checked, it will be deleted, which we expect to occur within about one month of your session, and it will be retained for no longer than one year after the dissertation defense that concludes this doctoral study.

If your information includes personal details, we will separate and destroy them after the study is complete. This informed consent form will be retained for 3 years after the study is complete, then destroyed. Your de-identified data, which cannot be traced back to you, may be retained indefinitely and used or distributed in future research studies.

It's very unlikely, but sometimes people who ensure research is conducted safely may ask to see your study information. This would only happen if the law or research rules require us to share it. Also, if a court orders us to share study materials or data, we may have to give them to the court, and your information may no longer be private.

Voluntary Nature of the Study

Being in this study is completely your choice. You will not get in trouble or be punished if you decide not to join. You can also stop being in the study at any time, even if you already said yes, and you can ask us to destroy anything we have collected from you up to that point. The other option is simple: you don't have to participate. You can skip any part of the session and stop at any time. Your decision whether or not to participate in this study will not affect your employment, your certification, or your standing with any airport, employer, or the university.

Your Scenario

You are the pilot flying a Piper Warrior, a low-wing airplane, on a series of recorded final approaches into Braden Airpark, a small general aviation airport in Easton, Pennsylvania, in day visual conditions. For each approach, fly it as you normally would: keep your eyes outside, monitor the instruments, listen to the radio, and stay aware of what is happening around the aircraft. You will report aloud anything you see or hear that matters, just as you would think out loud in the cockpit.

Your call sign for the session is:

WARRIOR FOUR-ONE-FIVE-THREE-THREE

When you hear a radio call directed to Warrior Four-One-Five-Three-Three, respond aloud as you would in flight.

Notice to Air Missions (mock, for this study)

!UAS 06/001 BRADEN AIRPARK (N43), EASTON PA UNMANNED AIRCRAFT SYSTEM (UAS) ACTIVITY MAY BE ENCOUNTERED IN THE VICINITY OF THE AIRPORT AND ALONG FINAL APPROACH. PILOTS ADVISED TO MAINTAIN VIGILANCE AND SEE-AND-AVOID. ALT SFC-3000FT AGL. DLY 0800-1800 LCL. !N43 06/002 RWY 36 PAPI OTS, INDICATIONS UNRELIABLE.

This is a simulated NOTAM created for the study. It tells you that drones may be operating in the area, the same kind of advisory you might see before a real flight. It does not tell you whether any particular approach will contain a drone. A second notice advises that the runway 36 precision approach path indicator (PAPI) is out of service with unreliable indications; judge your glidepath visually. Note also: the pilot you see in the video is flying the airplane and exercising normal crew resource management. He may look around, gesture, or point, sometimes at traffic, sometimes not, just as happens on any flight deck. Do not treat his movements as a signal; report only what you see with your own eyes.

What to Watch For and Report Aloud

1. Any unmanned aircraft (drone) you see, as soon as you see it: press the controller trigger right away, then point the controller at where you saw it and press the trigger again to lock it in. Also say aloud where it is relative to your aircraft. If you press by accident, point at the red panel behind you and press; it is simply noted, and the flight continues.

2. Any time the approach drifts outside normal tolerances (see the reference below).

3. Any radio call directed to your call sign, Warrior Four-One-Five-Three-Three. Respond aloud.

Approach Tolerances to Monitor

Treat these as the normal limits for a stabilized approach. Call out aloud if the approach goes outside any of them.

Airspeed: within about 10 knots of the target approach speed.
Glidepath: on or near the normal glidepath, not noticeably high or low.
Centerline: lined up with the runway, not drifting left or right of the centerline.
Descent: a steady descent, not a sudden sink or climb.

Post-session interview

Semi-structured, 30 to 45 minutes, immediately after the final scenario. Organised around the three fidelity dimensions from Rehmann et al. (1995): equipment, environment and psychological. Open-ended questions, follow-ups that respond to what they raise, and room for anything the protocol did not anticipate. Stay neutral.

The fifteen questions below are Appendix A, Semi-Structured Interview Protocol v0.1, as submitted with the approved application (IRB-26-321, approved 1 July 2026). The probes under each question are from the same document; use them as needed to follow up on what the participant raises. The protocol allows reordering or rewording for clarity and follow-up questions on anything unanticipated. Stay neutral: do not hint at a preferred answer, agree or disagree, or use terms the participant has not used. When an answer is short or unclear, use a neutral prompt ("Tell me more about that", "What was that like for you", "What do you mean by that") and allow a few seconds of silence first. If they report discomfort, dizziness or eye strain, note it, remind them the session may be stopped, and pause if needed.

Post-session debriefing

Debriefing Script v0.4 (8 September 2026). Read to the participant at the end of the session, after the interview and before they leave, adjusting the wording to sound natural. The participant already learned the study's purpose at consent, so this confirms and explains it. The only detail held back during the task was which scenarios contained a target.

"That is the end of the session. I want to go over what this study was looking at, the same purpose you read about when you signed the consent form. The study is testing whether this virtual cockpit setup can be used to study how pilots visually detect small drones from the cockpit, by comparing how often pilots spot them here against detection rates from earlier studies that used real flights.

You may have noticed that I did not tell you ahead of time which approaches had a drone, how many did, or when one would appear. Some did, and some did not. I held that back on purpose, because if you had known which ones to expect, you would have searched differently than you normally would in flight, and the point is to see how pilots scan when they are not sure anything is there. That was the only detail not shared during the task. Nothing about the study or what you were asked to do was misrepresented.

One more thing about what you saw. The camera that recorded these flights cannot resolve an object as small as a drone at the distances involved; it sees about three times less finely than your eye does. So on the approaches that had a drone, the drone was drawn back into the recording at exactly the position and path the flight data recorded, at the size your eye would have seen it from the cockpit. Nothing else in the video was changed, and one of the drone approaches was left exactly as the camera recorded it. In a moment I can show you a short video of each drone as it came into view, with an arrow pointing to it and a close-up, so you can see where they were.

Your responses, including the times of your controller presses and where you pointed, are kept under a code rather than your name and stored securely, as described in the consent form. You can ask me anything now, and if you decide you would rather not have your data included, tell me, and I will remove it. In a few weeks, if you agree, I will send you a short summary of the themes from your interview so you can check that I captured what you meant. Do you have any questions?"

If yes: remove their data, record it here and in the study records, and tell them it has been done.
